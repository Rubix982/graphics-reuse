## The best few, in more depth

### 1. HOOD (CVPR 2023): the one usable starting point

**The work it avoids redoing.** Simulating or training per garment. One network, trained once with a physics loss and no simulation data, predicts each time step for garments, body shapes and materials it has not seen. Material stiffness, bending and density are per-node and per-edge inputs, so they can be set at inference. As used by the paper this is across many instances, and it changes the answer: the network approximates an implicit-Euler step, and its result is not a solver's. As a warm start for the test bed's own solver it would be across runs, with `reuse_changes` depending on whether Blender settles from HOOD's output into the same fold pattern it reaches from the flat pattern (the T-005 question).

**Numbers it reports.** Table 1, a 12K-vertex VTO dress: the three-level model runs at 7.27 and 13.6 fps in two columns (the extract did not make clear what the columns mean); single-level baselines run at 6.65/13.1 fps (15 steps) and 2.45/4.99 fps (48 steps). The main text gives no hardware, and the supplement was blocked. In a perceptual study (30 participants, 8 sequences) preference between HOOD and ARCSim was "nearly equal" on the validation sequences; ARCSim was dropped on the new sequences because it failed to resolve self-collisions. Third-party timings: 25.22–49.52 ms per frame on an RTX A6000 for 2.6k–8.1k-vertex garments (HyperBones, Table 2); 125.5 ms per frame and 10 h of training, hardware not stated (OneFit, Table 7); 1,017 ms and 750 ms per frame on two phone CPUs for a 4,424-vertex T-shirt, with HOOD unsupported on the phone GPUs and the runtime framework not stated (Arm blog, 2025-06-06). Training data: 52 AMASS sequences, SMPL shapes sampled from U(−2, 2), six garment templates (shirt, tank top, long-sleeve top, shorts, pants, dress) resized by U(0.9, 1.1), materials sampled log-uniformly. The inference config in the repo samples Lamé μ in 15,909–63,636, λ in 3,535–93,334, bending in 6.4e-8 to 1.3e-3 and density in 0.0434–0.7 (`configs/aux/from_any_pose.yaml`; the config does not give units).

**Conditions the authors state.** Locality makes the model "agnostic to both the global structure and shape of the garment". Unseen garments are shown qualitatively (FoldSketch garments, a skirt made in Blender, a garment from a scan). The only quantitative comparison uses one dress.

**Failure modes and limits they state.** "Our model does not handle garment-garment interactions and our results may therefore exhibit self-penetrations." It "may fail when body motions exceed the velocities seen at training time." Bodies with severe self-intersections lead to wrong body–garment correspondences. With too few message-passing steps garments look rubbery and over-stretched. The repo's any-pose notebook warns that pinned vertices are "not yet supported in this example", so lower-body garments "would slide down". `datasets/from_any_pose.py` now reads pinned vertex types, which may have fixed this (untested).

**What it would take under the constraints.**
1. Environment: PyTorch CPU or MPS wheels, `torch_geometric`, pytorch3d (used only for `knn_points` in the criterions and runners), and the `smplx` pip package. Then change `device` from `'cuda:0'` to `'cpu'` in the config. pytorch3d ops have no MPS path, so expect CPU (reasoned, untested).
2. Data: the aux data and weights come from a Google Drive link, with no account needed. SMPL needs a registered download, but the mesh-sequence path with `smpl_model` unset never loads it (`datasets/from_any_pose.py`, lines 57–61; code reading, untested). The test bed's own body would be passed in as a mesh sequence.
3. Inputs: an assembled 3D garment `.obj` aligned with the first body frame. HOOD starts after sewing; it cannot close seams from flat panels.
4. The obstacle for a fitting loop: the network reads rest edge vectors from `rest_pos` (`models/postcvpr.py`, lines 149–171), and the any-pose loader sets `rest_pos` to the garment's first-frame positions (`datasets/from_any_pose.py`, line 163). The rest state is therefore a 3D mesh, not the flat pattern. A cut change of millimetres to centimetres would have to be turned into new 3D rest edge vectors, which the code does not do (code reading).
5. Use: as a warm start. Roll HOOD forward to a near-rest drape of the new cut, load that as Blender cloth's starting shape, and let Blender finish. Check it by METHOD.md step 4 against the fresh drapes' seed-to-seed spread.

Expected cost, reasoned from the phone timing and not measured: at 0.75–1.0 s per frame at 4.4k vertices, a 200-frame roll-out on a laptop CPU is minutes, not seconds. Any saving has to come from Blender needing fewer frames after the warm start, not from HOOD being nearly free.

**Test-bed problems it addresses.** A new pattern per design: yes, that is its central claim, though only one garment was measured. Drape time and drapes that never settle: only as a warm start, untested. Smooth folds: no; it runs at the given mesh resolution. Free-hanging scarf over the tunic: no, since it has no cloth-on-cloth contact. Very wide trousers: unknown; pants in its training set are fitted.

### 2. ContourCraft (SIGGRAPH 2024): the multi-layer version, CUDA-bound

**The work it avoids redoing.** The same as HOOD, plus resolving intersections between layers without needing an intersection-free start. Across many instances; the answer.

**Numbers it reports.** Table 2, RTX 4090, time step 1/30 s: 10.49 fps for 2 garments with 12,030 nodes; 10.18 fps for 2 garments with 14,003; 9.33 fps for 3 with 17,745; 6.2 fps for 3 with 20,218; 3.66 fps for 5 with 36,515 (some outfits were downsampled to fit on one GPU). Table 1, average penetrating triangle pairs over all outfits: HOOD 8,796, repulsion only 4,424, without the intersection-contour loss 391, ContourCraft 150. Perceptual study, 8 sequences: realism on a 1–5 scale was CLO3D 4.29, ContourCraft 4.15, linear blend skinning 3.04, HOOD 2.78 (22 raters per video); against CLO3D side by side, ContourCraft was preferred 35.8% of the time (37 raters per pair). Training time and GPU are not stated.

**Conditions the authors state.** Trained in three stages on simple single-layer garments plus three multi-layer BEDLAM outfits. The unseen test is five more BEDLAM outfits (40 validation sequences). Garments start from linear blend skinning at the first frame and may begin with severe self-penetrations, which the method is built to recover from.

**Failure modes and limits they state.** Some intersection configurations are "difficult to resolve due to the nature of the Intersection Contour loss". Small non-manifold pieces such as pockets "may pop outside the garment in a single frame", after which the model treats that as correct. Entangled garments whose order cannot be inferred fail. Open-contour intersections (mesh boundaries passing through another surface) are not handled. Loose garments are not evaluated.

**What it would take under the constraints.** It does not run as shipped. INSTALL.md builds CCCollisions against NVIDIA's cuda-samples and installs RAPIDS cudf/cugraph 24.12 and cupy. In the source, `models/ccraft.py` imports Warp proximity queries (Warp has a CPU backend; reasoned), `runners/utils/impulses.py` imports `cccollisions`, and `utils/icontour.py` (the training loss) imports cudf, cugraph and cccollisions. Inference would need CCCollisions' CUDA intersection queries replaced with CPU code, and I did not estimate that effort. SMPL or SMPL-X need registration; a mesh-sequence notebook exists.

**Test-bed problems it addresses.** The free-hanging scarf over the tunic needs cloth-on-cloth contact, and this is the only released pretrained model in the area that provides it. But a scarf's free edges passing through the tunic are the open-contour case the authors say they don't handle (my mapping, untested). Otherwise it addresses the same problems as HOOD.

### 3. GDSR, Neural Garment Dynamic Super-Resolution (SIGGRAPH Asia 2024): detail on a coarse drape

**The work it avoids redoing.** Simulating at high resolution. It runs a cheap coarse simulation, and a network corrects the coarse shape and adds fine wrinkle residuals: a mesh graph net computes features from the coarse dynamics and body contact, a decoder predicts corrections, and a hypernetwork builds a per-triangle implicit wrinkle field. One network serves unseen garments, motions and body shapes. Across resolutions (and many instances); the answer.

**Numbers it reports.** On a 12th-gen Intel Core i7 with an RTX 3090, unoptimised PyTorch: a 14,528-vertex dress took 0.014 s of network time plus 0.045 s of low-resolution Marvelous Designer simulation per frame, against 0.194 s for the high-resolution simulation (about 3.3×, my division). A 43,257-vertex dress took 0.032 s plus 0.083 s, about 0.115 s per frame, against 0.559 s (about 4.9×). The network is 65 MB, independent of mesh size. Training used 5 outfits (including a pleated short skirt, a double-layered dress and a triple-lace long skirt) on Swing and House dances on one SMPL body, for about 300 epochs; training time and GPU are not reported. The unseen tests used 5 outfits (including a single-layer long dress), 7 unseen motions with walks rolled out to 1,500 frames, and varied body shapes.

**Conditions the authors state.** Coarse simulation at a particle distance of 30 mm and fine at 10 mm, both in Marvelous Designer; the output has about 10× the input's vertices; trained on a single fabric (silk charmeuse). Training garments had 1,161–5,010 low-resolution vertices, which brackets the test bed's piece size (about 5,000).

**Failure modes and limits they state.** "GDSR fails to capture wrinkle details influenced by fabric friction." It is "trained on a single fabric material." "Due to low-resolution collision detection, some collisions remain unresolved."

**What it would take under the constraints.** The code is AGPL-3.0, which is fine for private use and carries obligations only if the code is distributed or served. The checkpoint and training data are on Google Drive. The Python side is plain PyTorch, so CPU is plausible (reasoned). The C++ preprocessing (UV sampling, geometry-to-UV maps) is set up for a Windows MSVC build of Embree 3.5.2 and OpenCV 4.6.0 and would need a macOS build. Training on new data needs paired coarse and fine simulations in Marvelous Designer, which is commercial and needs an account; the released checkpoint avoids that. The real question is domain gap: the test bed's coarse input would come from Blender cloth, with different fabrics, not from Marvelous Designer at 30 mm (untested). The model is a dynamic roll-out with history, so a static drape would be fed in as a short settling sequence (reasoned).

**Test-bed problems it addresses.** Smooth folds, directly, if the smoothness comes from resolution rather than from fabric parameters or collision thickness. That should be tested first with one fine Blender drape. Drape time too, if coarser drapes plus the network replace fine ones.

### 4. ClothTransformer (2026): a second permissive pretrained simulator

**The work it avoids redoing.** A per-step collision-handling solve for meshes of any resolution. One model covers body-driven garments, robotic manipulation and object collisions. Across many instances; the answer.

**Numbers it reports.** Training took about 300 H200 GPU hours (160k pretraining steps plus 40k fine-tuning steps with a differentiable collision loss). Forward pass on one RTX 4090: 22.24 ms at 5k vertices, 43.03 ms at 10k, 90.87 ms at 20k, 275.27 ms at 40k. About 10 iterations of collision post-processing add about 30 ms. The paper compares this with about 10 s per frame for the GIPC solver on the same scenario. Data: 2,056 sequences of 240 frames. The human-garment subset is 56 sequences (about 13.4k frames) built from 14 cloth meshes (T-shirts and skirts) and 5 collision meshes. Raw mean vertex error on human garments is 6.92 cm, and the raw self-collision rate is 9.12%.

**Conditions the authors state.** All data use one material (stretch modulus 10⁶ Pa, bending 10⁵ Pa, density 200 g/m², friction 0.4), and the model infers material implicitly. Roll-outs start from the ground-truth first frame. The 8:1:1 split is random within each subset, so the paper does not clearly hold garment meshes out of training.

**Failure modes and limits they state.** "Currently, our model infers material properties implicitly." "In extremely challenging scenarios, single-step autoregressive (AR) predictions of highly nonlinear physical dynamics can still lead to considerable error accumulation"; one extreme object case reaches about 37 cm. Topology changes are not handled.

**What it would take under the constraints.** MIT licence. Its requirements are only torch≥2.1, numpy, einops, omegaconf, tensorboard and huggingface_hub, and grep finds CUDA only in the distributed-training setup. The checkpoint `ct-no-ccd-preview.pt` is on a public Hugging Face repo. CPU or MPS inference is plausible but untested, and the garment and body would have to be converted into its trajectory format.

**Test-bed problems it addresses.** Weakly. The fabric cannot be set, and the garment set is narrow. A 6.92 cm mean vertex error is far larger than the millimetre-to-centimetre cut changes a fitting loop has to resolve, so it cannot be a fitting surrogate; at most it could be a warm start, as HOOD could.

### 5. PhysGraph (2023): the technique, and why patch training generalises

**The work it avoids redoing.** Integrating a high-resolution cloth state. Forces are cheap to evaluate in parallel; integrating them is not. A learned integrator turns forces from an opaque force module into displacements. Applied to clothing, it subdivides a coarse garment ×16 (linear interpolation) and relaxes it to an approximate quasi-static equilibrium in K = 5 iterations of M = 10 message-passing steps. Across resolutions; the answer.

**Numbers it reports.** On an RTX A6000 with a CUDA implementation, the force module takes 0.037–0.078 ms and the integration module 7.1 ms (10³ edges), 9.7 ms (10⁴), 37.7 ms (10⁵) and 343 ms (10⁶). The authors "don't claim performance improvements compared to state-of-the-art."

**Conditions the authors state.** It was trained only on small patches cut from one simulated T-shirt, with stretch and bending forces only, fixed patch boundaries, and no supervision (it minimises the potential). It generalises at inference to other topologies, resolutions, materials and unseen collision forces. Results include a hood, input from linear blend skinning, and a multi-layer tucked-in outfit.

**Failure modes and limits they state.** "Like most other methods, our current model is unable to resolve pre-existing self-intersections." It runs per frame without temporal information, so results are "mostly temporally coherent with the exception of areas with clustered collisions."

**What it would take under the constraints.** No code was found, so only the technique is available. The reuse (coarse drape, subdivide, relax at fine resolution) rebuilds in Blender with the built-in solver doing the relaxing: subdivide the settled coarse drape and run a short cloth simulation. That is the existing coarse-to-fine entry; the network only replaces the relaxing solver.

**Test-bed problems it addresses.** Smooth folds, through added resolution. The other lesson bears on the per-garment problem: training on local patches with a physics energy generalises to any garment because the physics is local. That is the same reason HOOD generalises, and it is the property to look for in any future candidate.

## What the area doesn't offer

Nothing found from 2023–2026 uses a learned drape as the initial guess for a physical cloth solver and reports how much settling time it saves. The only design of that kind is Shao et al. (2020, background), for PBD and rods. It is also the only design where the reuse would change only the cost, which is the variant the test bed's fitting loop most wants. No neural simulator takes a 2D sewing pattern as its rest state. HOOD, ContourCraft, SENC and Pb4U-GNet condition on a 3D template mesh; DrapeNet and ISP use latent codes of closed garment families; DiffusedWrinkles uses three design parameters on one T-shirt template. So a surrogate that responds to a millimetre cut change exists only per template, and none models the sewing stage: all start from an assembled 3D garment. Static equilibrium is almost never evaluated. Methods are either dynamic roll-outs or one-shot quasi-static predictors with no convergence measure, and none reports how often a drape settles. Free-hanging cloth with open edges lying on another layer is unaddressed. ContourCraft explicitly cannot handle open-contour intersections, HOOD has no cloth-on-cloth contact, SENC needs meshes that can be closed, and no paper evaluates a scarf. Very wide trousers are also unevaluated: training trousers are fitted, and OneFit reports between-leg artifacts on pants. Generalisation to unseen garments is shown on small sets (one dress quantitatively for HOOD, five BEDLAM outfits for ContourCraft, 30 tops for PhysDrape, two garment types for Pb4U-GNet). Materials are sampled ranges rather than measured fabrics; only Fabric-101 fits measured fabrics, and that is a differentiable simulator, not a learned drape. CPU timings are almost absent: the one found (Arm, 2025) puts HOOD at 0.75–1.0 s per frame on phone CPUs at 4.4k vertices, so "orders of magnitude faster than simulation" is a GPU claim. Most training pipelines need SMPL and AMASS, which require registration. HOOD's mesh-sequence path avoids that at inference, but retraining would not.
