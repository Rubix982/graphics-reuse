# R-015 · Area B: fast cloth solvers and GPU simulation

Researcher, 2026-11-14. Scope: fast cloth solvers 2023–2026 (2022 and older only as marked background), judged against one Apple Silicon Mac with 16 GB, no NVIDIA GPU, free and open code, no accounts, no cloud. The test bed's problems for this area: Blender's built-in cloth solver takes 4–7 minutes per drape on pieces of about 5,000 vertices; many drapes don't settle within a 200-frame cap; very wide flared trousers get pinched between the stepping legs in a walk; a long free-hanging scarf; layers colliding (tunic over trousers, scarf over tunic).

How to read the numbers: every number below is quoted from the paper, project page or official repo named in its row, with the hardware the source gives. "Abstract only" means only the abstract or a project page was read. Where I did arithmetic on a paper's numbers it is marked (reasoned). Nothing here was run or measured.

## The best few, in more depth

### 1. Offset Geometric Contact on Vertex Block Descent (and its 2026 follow-up, Divide and Truncate), as implemented in Newton

**Work it avoids redoing.** Two kinds. Inside each step, OGC gives every vertex a conservative displacement bound when contacts are detected; while every vertex stays inside its bound the contact set is known to still be valid, so detection is not repeated. "DCD is only necessary when points reach their conservative bounds, which does not happen at every iteration, especially in the later stages of the optimization." It also removes continuous collision detection altogether. Across steps, VBD starts each step from x + h·v + h²·ã, where ã is gravity scaled by the previous frame's acceleration along gravity; "For resting contact, it keeps the previous position."

**Numbers, with hardware.** OGC paper: CPU version in C++ on an AMD Ryzen 7950X with 64 GB (threading not stated); GPU version in NVIDIA Warp on an RTX 4090. A dress of 14 stitched pieces, 15.7K vertices and 29.4K triangles, on a skeleton-driven body of 12.8K vertices, contact radius 2 mm, h = 1/200 s, 20 iterations: 30/42 ms per step (average/maximum) on the CPU, 1.2/1.4 ms on the GPU. At 200 steps per simulated second that is 6.0 s of CPU time per simulated second (reasoned, from those numbers). The same scene in the C-IPC paper is quoted as 24 s per 0.04 s frame; OGC reports 0.24 s on the CPU and 9.6 ms on the GPU. Cloth of 4.9K vertices dropped on a sphere: CPU VBD-OGC "more than about 128 times faster than IPC", Newton's-method OGC 9.2 times. Fifty layers of cloth on a cylinder: 0.21/0.55 s per step on CPU, 6.3/11.5 ms on GPU (the figure says 246K vertices, Table 1 says 1M; the paper disagrees with itself). Against GIPC on a twisting volumetric mat (GPU): 5.51 ms against 1,893 ms average per step, "343 times faster". VBD paper (Ryzen 5950X, RTX 4090): a 2,500-vertex tearing cloth at h = 1/300, 20 iterations, 11.2/11.5 ms per step on CPU; against XPBD in one comparison, 0.031 s against 0.32 s per frame.

**Conditions the authors state.** OGC needs the conservative relaxation factor set low enough "to ensure the conservative bounds remain conservative with floating point rounding errors" (γp = 0.45 in all their runs). VBD is stable at one iteration per step but "converges slower for stiffer materials" and "struggles with high stiffness ratios" (the motivation for AVBD). VBD needs a vertex colouring (Newton: `builder.color()`).

**Failure modes and limits they state.** VBD alone "cannot guarantee penetration-free results". OGC prevents cloth-cloth crossings, but with a skeleton-driven body "body-cloth penetration might occur during the simulation, but cloth-cloth penetration is prevented". OGC is slower than IPC's CCD line search "in cases with few collisions and large velocities", because each vertex may move less than half the distance to its nearest neighbour primitive per iteration, "and the issue worsens as velocity increases". Contact force is discontinuous at the open boundary of concave regions; "we did not observe any such issues". Every result was checked with an intersection analysis after every frame. Divide and Truncate (abstract only) states that animated objects are handled inside the guarantee and that a planar variant reduces "artificial damping and deadlock"; no numbers in the abstract.

**What it would take here.** Newton (Apache-2.0) installs with pip on macOS, CPU only. Its `SolverVBD` implements VBD, AVBD, OGC and Divide-and-Truncate self-contact. The catch is speed: Warp's documentation says "Currently, kernels launched on CPU devices will be executed in serial", so Newton on this Mac uses one core, and the paper's CPU numbers (a different C++ implementation, unknown threading) do not transfer. The first step would be one timed run of a 5,000-vertex test-bed piece through `SolverVBD` with self-contact on, with the garment and body exported from Blender and the result read back as a point cache. If that is too slow, Newton's Warp kernels are open (Apache-2.0) and could be ported to a Metal-capable Python compiler (Taichi or Quadrants, both Apache-2.0), or the per-colour updates vectorised in numpy on the CPU (reasoned, a sizeable job either way). It cannot run inside Blender's own cloth modifier.

**Test-bed problems it addresses.** Layers (tunic over trousers, scarf over tunic): cloth-cloth crossings are prevented by construction. The 4–7 minute drapes: possibly, depending on the serial-CPU measurement. The free-hanging scarf is the case OGC says it handles worst (few contacts, high velocity). The pinch between the legs is not addressed by OGC; Divide and Truncate claims animated objects but was read at abstract level only.

### 2. Domain-decomposed projective dynamics on a multicore CPU (Lu et al., SIGGRAPH 2025)

**Work it avoids redoing.** Each domain's projective-dynamics matrix is factorised once and the factorisations are reused every step; only the small interface problem between domains is solved per iteration, in parallel. Each step also begins with a "collision-free warm start" (local-global iterations without contact) before the collision-aware solve (the L and G columns of Table 1).

**Numbers, with hardware.** AMD Ryzen Threadripper PRO 5975WX (32 cores, 32 threads, CHOLMOD) and Intel i9-13900K (24 cores, MKL). Garments on moving avatars at h = 1/120: 376K, 390K, 315K and 519K degrees of freedom (about 105K–173K vertices) at 296, 395, 351 and 374 ms per frame. The multi-layered long dress (315K DOFs, 89 domains): average 351 ms per frame, maximum 626 ms. A kick in a tiered skirt (444K DOFs): 588 ms per step, "2.6× faster than GPU-based PD-BFGS and 66× faster than CPU-based C-IPC". Against multithreaded CPU PD with Coulomb friction on a cloth-on-Armadillo drape (282K DOFs): "11× faster under the same convergence condition and time step size". Against Lan et al. 2024 on GPU (1.1M DOFs, fashion show): slower, 1.06 s against 734 ms per step. Penetration-free when a C-IPC-style CCD line-search filter is applied at the end of each step, as the paper does for the knot and the stacked-tablecloth scenes.

**Conditions the authors state.** Enough domains to match the cores: "if the mesh is only decomposed into four domains, pushing the simulation to 16 or 32 threads is not helping". The materials must suit projective dynamics (quadratic constraint energies); nonlinear models "fundamentally" complicate the prefactorisation. "Our approach assumes that all CPU cores have similar computational capabilities and subdivides the mesh into domains of roughly equal sizes."

**Failure modes and limits they state.** "It does not explicitly address collision handling"; collision detection "represents a significant portion of the total computation time" on the CPU. Heterogeneous cores (performance plus efficiency) are named as unsolved, and the authors attribute some of their Intel results to exactly that.

**What it would take here.** No code was found (project page and paper have no link). A rebuild from the paper means a graph partitioner, per-domain sparse Cholesky, the primal-dual interface solve, the per-domain Gauss-Seidel local step and a CCD filter: weeks, research-grade. Apple Silicon has mixed performance and efficiency cores, which is the case the paper says it does not handle. The garments it reports are 20–35 times larger than a test-bed piece, so its per-frame times say little about 5,000 vertices. Also reasoned and untested: the prefactorisation depends on the mesh and on rest shape for triangle strain constraints, so a fitting loop that changes the cut would refactorise every run; for pure edge springs the PD matrix does not depend on rest lengths, so a cut change that keeps topology and only changes lengths would keep the factorisation (Liu et al. 2013 structure; check before relying on it).

**Test-bed problems it addresses.** The cost of a drape on a CPU-only machine is the problem it was written for. Layers are shown (multi-layered dress). Nothing specific on settling, pinching or free-hanging pieces.

### 3. Progressive Simulation for Cloth Quasistatics (2022, background) and Progressive Shell Quasistatics (2023)

**Work it avoids redoing.** It does not simulate the fall at all: it solves for the resting drape directly, at a very coarse level first, and starts each finer level from a safe prolongation of the coarser solution. The coarse levels are made consistent with the fine one by evaluating the fine mesh's membrane and bending energies during the coarse solve, so the folds found early survive refinement. The solve can be interrupted and resumed when a design parameter changes.

**Numbers, with hardware.** A cloth draped on a dragon, 370K triangles at the finest of five levels, on a MacBook M1 Max with 64 GB: PCS 122 s, C-IPC 1,248 s (10.2×). That C-IPC baseline already uses a trick the paper introduces, single-iteration time steps for IPC quasistatic solves, which on its own gave C-IPC "an order of magnitude speed-up"; against C-IPC's standard quasistatic stepping PCS was "well over two orders of magnitude" faster. A coarse preview with the enriched energy is on average 56% slower than a plain coarse solve and converges 14% faster. PSQ's page claims a coarse proxy "over two orders of magnitude faster to simulate than its detailed counterpart" (no hardware given).

**Conditions the authors state.** Built on C-IPC, so intersection-free and strain-limited at every level. The consistency of coarse and fine folds is observed, not guaranteed: "we have no guarantee nor proof that PCS will do so".

**Failure modes and limits they state.** Highly twisted or crumpled cloth, "for which coarse triangulations may miss fine-mesh wrinkling behavior". "In practice cloths (e.g., clothing) will often have fine-scale domain boundaries and elaborate seam constraints, which we do not currently handle." PCS needed Loop-subdivision hierarchies; PSQ lifts that to unstructured meshes, and its page says nothing about seams. The paper also shows what a naive cascade does (its "Sensitive Couture" baseline, a coarse solve used to start a fine one): coarse membrane-locking artifacts are passed up to the finest level, or the coarse folds are lost and new ones appear.

**What it would take here.** No code for either paper. Rebuilding the enriched coarse energy and safe prolongation on top of IPC Toolkit (MIT, CPU) or C-IPC (Apache-2.0) is research work, and seams are unhandled. Two cheaper things carry over. First, a drape whose only purpose is its resting shape can be stopped on a residual rather than a frame count. Second, the paper's evidence against naive cascades bears directly on the library's coarse-to-fine entry: a coarse Blender drape used to start a fine one is the cascade PCS shows failing, with no enriched energy to prevent it. The single-iteration-step trick applies to IPC-family solvers only, not to Blender's cloth.

**Test-bed problems it addresses.** Drapes that don't settle within 200 frames: a quasistatic solve has no frame cap. The cost of repeated drapes in a fitting loop: the interruptible solve is designed for parameter changes, though no measurement of that is reported. It does not address the walk.

### 4. GarmentCodeData's settling rule and failure record

**Work it avoids redoing.** Not reuse: it stops simulating once the drape has stopped moving, and records what goes wrong when draping many generated garments with an XPBD solver.

**Numbers, with hardware.** "If a small enough percentage of mesh vertices (1.5%) change their location between the frames by a magnitude greater than a specified threshold (0.04 cm), the mesh is considered static." Each drape is capped at 5 minutes and 2,400 frames; failing to reach rest marks a failure. Garments average about 30k triangles on a 47.5k-triangle body; on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 a drape takes 30 s on average. The success rate, counting pattern-mesh failures and quality control, is "about 72%". The first few frames run without gravity so the stitches can close.

**Conditions the authors state.** The rule is stated for their solver and their frame rate; no sensitivity analysis of the two thresholds is reported.

**Failure modes and limits they state.** Sliding: bulky, heavy garments with no grip on the body keep falling. Heavy self-intersection of a skirt with a long upper piece. Body-cloth misalignment from the initial placement (a skirt caught in the feet, cuffs sticking out). Limitations section: "lack of fabric layers", sharp pleats, hardware, full outfits.

**What it would take here.** Little: the rule can be computed in Blender's Python from the cloth's evaluated vertex positions frame by frame, or afterwards from the point cache, and used to stop or to label a drape as unsettled. Blender's gravity weight can be keyframed for gravity-free sewing frames. Whether stopping early changes the answer depends on the thresholds; the library's check (fresh drapes over several seeds) would decide it.

**Test-bed problems it addresses.** Drapes that don't settle within 200 frames: a stated, measurable definition of settled, from a pipeline that allowed 12 times as many frames. Its failure list (sliding, a long top crossing a skirt, initial placement) overlaps the test bed's.

### 5. Subspace reuse in Lan et al. 2024 (for the library; not usable here)

**Work it avoids redoing.** A low-frequency subspace is built once from the rest-pose system matrix (eigenvectors of the smallest eigenvalues) and reused every step, including after contact changes the matrix to H + ΔH, where the reduced system becomes (Λ + UᵀΔHU)q = Uᵀ(b − HX − ΔHX). The subspace solve clears the slow, garment-wide error that Jacobi iterations remove poorly.

**Numbers, with hardware.** Intel i7-12700 and NVIDIA RTX 3090; only GPU timings are reported. Subspace reuse cut the follow-up aggregated-Jacobi iterations "by 70% on average". A fashion-show garment of about 340K vertices at Δt = 1/200 s runs at 4.8 FPS. 12× faster than GPU PD-IPC on one scene; 130× faster than PD-BFGS on a knot. The per-vertex precomputation makes the subspace update "50 to 150 times faster" than a direct projection with cuBLAS.

**Conditions and limits they state.** The result is penetration-free because a full CCD and line-search filter run at the end of each step; the partial CCD inside is a sampled approximation. Subspace reuse is "less perfect" under heavy collision, and ΔH makes the reduced matrix dense, so the subspace must stay small (r = 30 during contact). Residual forwarding is a "heuristic treatment" that "becomes erroneous and generates artifacts" when the forwarded residual is large; with a 5-iteration cap a teapot showed "rubber-like dynamics".

**Why it is here.** It is the clearest named reuse inside a cloth solver in the window, with a measured effect and a stated condition under which it degrades. It is a candidate for its own entry under "across time", with MGPBD's lazy multigrid setup (reused for 20 frames, "up to 100 frames showed no noticeable convergence degradation") as a second source from a different solver family. Under this Mac's constraints it is unusable: GPU only and no code.

## What the area doesn't offer

The headline speedups in this area are almost all measured on an NVIDIA RTX 3090, RTX 4090 or L40S, and none transfer to this Mac. Only two cloth solvers in the window run natively on Apple GPUs: ppf-contact-solver, whose README calls its Metal and CPU backends slow, and Genesis's robotics-grade PBD. Newton puts VBD, OGC and Divide-and-Truncate within reach on macOS, but its CPU kernels run on one core, so its speed here is unknown until someone times it. Most of the fast methods also buy their speed with per-mesh precomputation reused across the time steps of one garment: a prefactorisation, a rest-pose subspace, a multigrid hierarchy, or JGS2's 4–77 minutes of cubature training. None of them measures what happens when the cut changes between runs, which is the test bed's fitting loop. PCS is the only paper that even designs for parameter changes, and it reports no measurement of that case. No paper in the window gives a convergence or settling criterion for dynamic drapes beyond dataset heuristics like GarmentCodeData's, and the quasistatic solvers that stop on a residual (PCS, PSQ) have no code and, in PCS's own words, don't handle seams. Nothing in 2023–2026 addresses cloth pinched between moving legs. The guaranteed solvers stop rather than cross: ppf's own article says a cloth pinched by an animated character can end with "Newton solve made no progress (over-constrained configuration)" and suggests softening the collider. OGC says body-cloth penetration may still occur with a skeleton-driven body. Baraff, Witkin and Kass (2003) remain the reference for pinching. A long free-hanging scarf (few contacts, high speed) is the case OGC names as its weak one, and no paper treats it specifically. Benchmarks of solvers on garments exist only inside method papers, on each paper's own scenes and hardware, or in robotics benchmarks of flat sheets. There is no independent comparison of drape accuracy and speed across solvers. Finally, Blender's own path forward, the 5.2 XPBD Geometry Nodes solver, lacks cloth self-collision according to its manual, so it can't do layers yet. And none of these methods can be put inside Blender 4.5's built-in cloth solver: they would have to run beside Blender, exchanging meshes and caches with it.
